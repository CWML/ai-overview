Literature Tools

Consensus, NotebookLM and other AI tools for finding and reading research

Last checked: November 18, 2026.

AI literature tools are good at getting you oriented quickly: what’s been studied, roughly what it found, and which papers to read first. They aren’t a substitute for a structured search, and they shouldn’t be the source you cite [1, 2]. Open and read every paper before you rely on it.

In beta: AI search of Yale Library’s catalog

Yale Library’s AI Connector lets AI assistants such as Claude and ChatGPT search almost 15 million records in the library catalog and nearly 185,000 in its digital collections. It’s in beta, and the Yale community is invited to test it. It doesn’t search licensed articles or article databases such as JSTOR and EBSCO; use Articles+ or the databases for those [3].

  • Which assistant: Yale doesn’t provide ChatGPT, so a personal ChatGPT account is for public information only [4]. For OpenAI’s models under your Yale account, Clarity is the alternative, but the connector doesn’t list Clarity yet; the library has named it only as a possible future option [5].
  • Get started: the AI Connector setup site (Yale sign-in).
  • Feedback: libraryfeedback@yale.edu

None of these AI tools gives reproducible, complete search results. For a systematic, scoping or other formal review, work with a CWML librarian on a documented database search [1, 2].

Consensus

Consensus searches more than 250 million peer-reviewed papers (from Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex and its own web crawl) and writes an answer with citations. Yale Library is running a year-long trial [6, 10].

Getting access

  • Go to consensus.app and sign up with your Yale email (yale.edu, ynhh.org or ynhh.com) or Yale Google login [6].
  • The Yale trial runs through December 31, 2026 [6, 10]. Ask the library (marx.reference@yale.edu) what happens after that.
  • Approved for low-risk data only. Ask questions about published research. Don’t paste unpublished results, draft manuscripts or participant data [6].

What it does

  • Search modes: Quick, Pro and Deep, which read roughly 10, 20 and 50 papers [6].
  • Consensus Meter: for yes/no questions (“Does X reduce Y?”), a bar showing how many papers lean Yes, Possibly, Mixed or No [2, 11].
  • Study snapshots: population, sample size, methods and outcomes pulled from a paper [2].
  • Filters: study type (RCT, meta-analysis, systematic review), journal quartile, sample size, year [2, 10].
  • Full text or abstract? A checkmark on a citation means Consensus read the full text. No checkmark means it worked from the abstract only.
  • Access to paywalled papers: links open through Yale’s subscriptions [2].

Know the limits

  • The Meter counts papers; it doesn’t weigh them. One large RCT and one small cross-sectional survey count the same. Published literature also over-represents positive results.
  • Paywalled papers are often summarized from the abstract alone.
  • Labels can be wrong. The study-type label is assigned by AI. Journal rank is not study quality.
  • Answers aren’t reproducible. The same question can give different answers in Pro and Deep mode, or on a different day.
  • Paid plans add more Pro and Deep searches. The free tier has monthly limits. Check current limits in the app.

These limits are discussed in Aaron Tay’s review of Consensus [2] and in CWML’s comparison of Consensus, OpenEvidence and UpToDate [11].

TipA good Consensus workflow
  1. Ask a specific question: population, exposure or intervention, outcome.
  2. Check the Meter, then filter to systematic reviews, meta-analyses or RCTs and see if the picture changes.
  3. Open the top 3–5 papers. Check that each one says what the summary claims.
  4. Note which answers came from abstracts only.
  5. Use what you learn to build a proper PubMed search.

NotebookLM

NotebookLM answers questions only from the sources you put in a notebook, and every answer links back to the passage it came from. It’s free with your Yale Google account and approved for moderate-risk data [7].

Good uses:

  • Compare the methods, samples and measures across 5–20 papers you’ve already chosen
  • Find where in a long report or guidance document something is discussed
  • Make a study guide or an audio overview of your lab’s papers for a new trainee
WarningWhat you may upload
  • Open-access papers (PubMed Central, preprints), your own manuscripts and your lab’s documents: yes, up to moderate risk.
  • PDFs you downloaded through Yale Library subscriptions: generally no. Publisher licenses usually forbid uploading their content to AI tools. See the library’s AI guidance for library resources [12].
  • Identifiable participant data: no. NotebookLM is approved for moderate risk only [7].
